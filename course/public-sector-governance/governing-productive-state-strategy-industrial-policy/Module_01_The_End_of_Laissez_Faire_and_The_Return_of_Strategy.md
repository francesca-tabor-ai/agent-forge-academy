# Module 1: The End of Laissez-Faire & The Return of Strategy

**Focus:** Understanding the theoretical shift in economic governance caused by AI.

## Learning Objectives

- Analyze why AI is a structural shock requiring new governance models
- Understand the limits of market stewardship for AI
- Define the "Productive State" and its role in AI governance
- Recognize the strategic choice between AI maker vs. AI taker

## Core Topics

### The Structural Shock of AI

**Why AI Is Not Just Digital:**

**General-Purpose Industrial Technology:**
- AI comparable to electricity or mechanization
- Reshapes entire production systems
- Not limited to digital services
- Fundamental economic transformation

**Comparable Technologies:**
- **Electricity:** Transformed manufacturing and society
- **Mechanization:** Changed production processes
- **AI:** Reshapes decision-making and automation

**Impact on Production Systems:**
- Changes how goods are produced
- Alters service delivery
- Transforms supply chains
- Reshapes economic structures

**Why This Matters:**
- Requires new governance models
- Demands strategic state intervention
- Cannot be left to markets alone
- Needs industrial policy approach

### The Limits of Market Stewardship

**Why Neutral Arbiter Model Fails:**

**Market Stewardship Model:**
- State as neutral arbiter
- Minimal intervention
- Market-led development
- Reactive regulation

**Why It's Insufficient for AI:**

**Scale Requirements:**
- AI rewards scale
- Large capital investments needed
- Network effects important
- Market alone may not provide

**Coordination Needs:**
- Multiple actors must align
- Infrastructure coordination required
- Data sharing necessary
- Standards need setting

**Long-Term Investment:**
- Returns take time
- High upfront costs
- Uncertain outcomes
- Market may underinvest

**Strategic Imperatives:**
- National security concerns
- Economic sovereignty
- Technological independence
- Competitive advantage

### Defining the "Productive State"

**Moving Beyond Risk Management:**

**Traditional State Role:**
- Risk management
- Safety regulation
- Ethics oversight
- Consumer protection

**Productive State Approach:**
- Governing for production
- Building capacity
- Strategic investment
- Industrial development

**Key Capabilities:**
- Design systems domestically
- Manufacture components
- Deploy at scale
- Maintain sovereignty

**Strategic Objectives:**
- Sovereign capability
- Economic resilience
- Technological independence
- Competitive advantage

### The "AI Maker vs. AI Taker" Dilemma

**The Strategic Choice:**

**AI Maker:**
- Site of AI-enabled production
- Develops AI systems
- Controls technology
- Creates value

**AI Taker:**
- Market for AI outputs
- Consumes AI services
- Depends on others
- Extracts value

**Implications:**
- **Maker:** Strategic advantage, sovereignty, value creation
- **Taker:** Dependence, vulnerability, value extraction

**The Choice:**
- Active industrial policy
- Strategic investment
- Capability building
- Or passive consumption

## Key Takeaways

1. **Structural shock:** AI is a general-purpose industrial technology requiring new governance
2. **Market limits:** Market stewardship insufficient for scale, coordination, and long-term investment
3. **Productive state:** Must govern for production, not just risk management
4. **Strategic choice:** Maker vs. taker determines economic sovereignty and competitive position

## Reflection Questions

1. Why is AI a "structural shock" comparable to electricity or mechanization?
2. How do AI's scale, coordination, and long-term investment needs challenge market stewardship?
3. What does it mean for the state to "govern for production" rather than just manage risk?
4. What are the strategic implications of being an AI maker vs. AI taker?
5. How does the productive state model differ from traditional industrial policy?
